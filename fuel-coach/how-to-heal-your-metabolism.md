# How to Heal Your Metabolism — Fuel Coach Reference

**Source:** Kate Deering, *How to Heal Your Metabolism* (2015). Deering is a CNC, a PT and a CHEK Holistic Lifestyle Coach.
**Lineage:** The book applies Ray Peat's "pro-metabolic" framework. Broda Barnes and Hans Selye are also major influences.
**Purpose of this file:** a reference the Fuel Coach can use to explain the book's model, protocols, food lists and recipes to clients.

---

## 0. How the coach should use this file

- **Attribute everything to the book.** These are Deering's (and Ray Peat's) positions, not settled consensus. Phrase them as "This approach holds that…" or "Deering recommends…".
- **Several positions go against mainstream nutrition guidance.** Present these as the book's view and don't overstate the evidence:
  - PUFAs, including fish oil and omega-3s, are harmful.
  - Leafy and cruciferous vegetables should be limited.
  - Fiber is often a problem.
  - More salt is protective.
  - White sugar and juice are good fuels.
  - Grains should be avoided while healing.
- **Refer to a medical provider** before a client changes anything involving:
  - medications (statins, thyroid medication, birth control, blood-pressure drugs)
  - salt intake with hypertension or kidney disease
  - diabetes or blood-sugar medication
  - pregnancy
  - kidney stones
  - eating-disorder history
  - large calorie increases in a medically complex client
- **Go slowly.** This is the book's own #1 rule: make one or two changes at a time. Adding food, sugar, salt or fat too fast causes weight gain, water retention or digestive upset.
- **Source gaps.** The text this file was built from was missing many bulleted lists, including every recipe's directions. Where a list was lost it is marked *[not in source]*. Recipe methods marked *(method reconstructed)* are standard preparations, not the author's wording.

## 1. The core model

### 1.1 Metabolism = cellular respiration
- A well-working metabolism takes glucose into the cell and burns it in the mitochondria to make ATP, **heat, CO₂ and water**.
- More glucose burned efficiently means more heat and energy, which means a higher metabolic rate.
- The book treats **"low thyroid" and "low metabolic rate" as the same thing**.
- The thyroid is "the conductor of every metabolic process."
- In the 1950s Broda Barnes estimated that up to 40% of Americans had low thyroid function. The author believes the figure is higher now, even when labs look normal.

### 1.2 Thyroid vs. stress hormones (the central see-saw)
- Thyroid hormones and the stress hormones (adrenaline, cortisol) are **antagonists**.
- When the thyroid is strong, stress hormones stay low. When the thyroid is sluggish or the body is under stress, adrenaline and cortisol rise to supply quick energy and heat.
- **Adrenaline** mobilizes glycogen and free fatty acids. It shunts blood to the brain and organs and away from the skin, which causes **cold hands, feet and nose**.
- **Cortisol** breaks down the body's own tissue (muscle, thymus, connective tissue) through **gluconeogenesis**: making glucose from amino acids, glycerol, lactate and pyruvate.
- **Glucagon** also raises blood sugar, by releasing liver glycogen and driving gluconeogenesis.
- This is useful short term and destructive when chronic.
- The book's summary principle: **health = high metabolism + low stress.**

### 1.3 What drives the metabolism down, per the book
- Under-eating and chronic dieting
- Low-carb diets
- Over-exercise, especially endurance and high-intensity work
- PUFA-rich oils
- Soy, grains, legumes and nuts
- Excess muscle meat
- Raw cruciferous vegetables
- Fasting and cleanses
- Too little sleep
- Chronic stress
- Overhydration
- Drugs and alcohol
- Unhappiness
- Environmental chemicals

### 1.4 Pro-thyroid vs. anti-thyroid inputs

| Pro-thyroid / pro-metabolic | Anti-thyroid / anti-metabolic |
|---|---|
| Saturated fats (coconut oil, butter, ghee) | Vegetable, seed, nut and fish oils (PUFA) |
| Sugars: fruit, OJ, honey, white sugar, milk | Processed foods, starches and grains |
| Salt | Soy, legumes, nuts and seeds |
| Easy-to-digest animal protein: dairy, eggs, shellfish, white fish, gelatin, broth, liver | Excess muscle meat; egg whites alone; whey |
| Sleep, rest, light/moderate exercise, happiness | Endurance events, over-training, high stress, sleep loss, radiation, drugs |

### 1.5 Mechanisms of a sluggish thyroid, as the book explains them

**Weight gain**
- Low T3 means less energy and heat are produced, so more is stored.
- A **1 °C drop in body temperature cuts metabolic rate by 10–13%**. On a 2,000 kcal maintenance diet that is about **200–230 kcal/day less** burned.

**High cholesterol**
- T3 plus vitamin A convert cholesterol into the steroid hormones: pregnenolone, progesterone, DHEA, testosterone, estrogen, cortisol and aldosterone.
- With low T3, cholesterol backs up in the blood.
- The book advises **checking thyroid before starting statins**. Refer this to a provider.

**Sex hormones**
- Under stress, available thyroid and cholesterol are diverted to cortisol, at the cost of sex hormones.
- Effects: low libido, PMS, irregular cycles, infertility, dry skin and hair, muscle loss.
- The book's formula: "Reduced stress = increased thyroid = increased sex hormones."

**Liver**
- The liver runs on its own glycogen and needs thyroid hormone to use it. It also does much of the T4 → T3 conversion.
- A sluggish liver clears less estrogen, insulin, ammonia, drugs and toxins.

**Digestion and immunity**
- Low metabolism means low stomach acid (HCl). That causes poor protein, mineral and B12 absorption, bacterial overgrowth, bloating and IBS-type symptoms.
- About 70% of the immune system is in the gut.
- Cortisol breaks down the thymus, which makes T-cells, so immunity drops.

**Blood pressure**
- Chronic stress keeps the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) switched on, which raises blood pressure.

**Mood**
- An early "cortisol high" eventually gives way to fatigue and depression.

**Sleep**
- Poor liver glycogen storage means blood sugar can't be held overnight.
- Adrenaline and cortisol rise, causing night waking and insomnia (see §9.2).

### 1.6 The "fat burner" myth
- The body always uses a mix of glucose, fat and protein.
- The book's view: **during activity most energy should come from glucose oxidation**, because it gives the most heat and CO₂.
- Training the body to be a "fat burner" makes it more efficient (slower) and impairs glucose metabolism.
- **At rest, muscle and heart prefer fat.** So **sleep and more muscle mass** are the "safest" ways to burn fat.

## 2. Assessing metabolic health

### 2.1 Signs of a healthy metabolism
These come from Barnes and the author:
- Warm body: **97.8–98.6 °F (36.6–37 °C)**
- Resting pulse **75–90 bpm** (Ray Peat)
- Energy all day; deep, restful sleep of **7–9 uninterrupted hours**
- Good digestion; **1–3 bowel movements a day**
- **Infrequent urination, about 4–6 times a day**
- Healthy skin, shiny hair, strong nails, bones and teeth
- Healthy libido and fertility; no PMS
- Clear mind; happy mood
- Lean and muscular without hours of exercise; rarely ill
- Blood pressure about 120/80

### 2.2 Signs the metabolism is "on the fritz"
- Always cold, often **95–97 °F**, especially hands, feet and nose
- **Pulse under 75 bpm**
- Urinating all day; thirst never quenched
- Constipation, bloating, gas
- Poor sleep, waking tired, fatigue, depression, foggy thinking
- Low or absent sex drive
- Dry skin, hair and nails; slow wound healing; frequent illness
- Gains weight easily, loses it with difficulty; muscle loss with fat gain

Even a few of these may point to a depressed metabolism. The author argues that lifestyle, stress and diet matter more than age.
*[The book's self-assessment questionnaire is not in the source.]*

### 2.3 Home tests (guides, not diagnoses)

**Basal temperature (Barnes protocol)**
1. Shake down a thermometer and leave it by the bed.
2. On waking, before moving, place it **in the armpit and lie still for 10 minutes**.
3. A healthy waking reading is **≥ 97.8 °F (36.6 °C)**.
4. Temperature should rise through the day to about **98.6 °F (37 °C) around midday**.

- A mercury thermometer is most accurate. An oral digital thermometer is acceptable; take several readings.
- **Falsely high readings:** fever, electric blanket, hot weather, hot drinks, **high cortisol or adrenaline**. A stressed person can read warm.
- **Falsely low readings:** starvation, a cold, cold drinks, cool weather, pituitary or adrenal problems.

**Pulse**
- Ideal resting pulse is **75–90 bpm**.
- A low pulse means fitness, not health. Endurance athletes often run **mid-40s to mid-50s bpm and 94.5–96 °F**, which the book calls adaptation to chronic stress.
- Blood-pressure problems, heart conditions, diabetes and cancer can change pulse. Use pulse together with temperature and symptoms.

**Post-meal check**
- Measure temperature and pulse **on waking** and again **20–30 minutes after each meal**.
- A supportive meal should raise both, and the person should feel **warm, alert and content**.

**Post-workout check**
- If temperature and pulse drop after training, the workout suppressed the thyroid: it was too long, too hard or too intense.
- Fix: shorten the session, rest more, or lower the intensity.

### 2.4 The log (the book's "single most useful step")
- Record each day:
  - meal times, amounts and macros
  - temperature and pulse (waking and after meals)
  - how each meal makes you feel
  - sleep
  - bowel movements
- Suggested tool: **Cronometer**.
- Logging shows under- or over-eating, macro imbalances and foods that drop temperature or pulse or affect mood and sleep.
- "If you are not logging you will only be guessing."

## 3. Macronutrient framework

### 3.1 Targets per meal and per day

| Macro | Range | Starting point | Adjust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 40–70% | ~50% | Start lower if inactive or more metabolically damaged. Go higher if athletic, young or high-metabolism. Raise as healing progresses. The author eats 55–60%, more when training. |
| Protein | 20–30% | ~25% | Slows the blood-sugar rise from carbs and extends satiety. Too much without carbs and fat triggers a stress response. |
| Fat | 20–30% | — | Damaged metabolisms need more fat, especially **women with hormonal issues** (lost period, infertility) or after long low-fat dieting. Lower fat and raise carbs as healing progresses. |

### 3.2 The golden pairing rule
- **Never eat carbohydrate alone, and never eat protein alone.** Every meal and snack should have carb + protein + fat.
- **Carb alone** (fruit, juice, sugar, even root vegetables) gives a fast spike and an insulin surge.
- **Protein alone** raises insulin, because insulin carries amino acids into cells. Without carbs, blood sugar drops, then adrenaline and cortisol rise and muscle is broken down. The usual sign is feeling light-headed after eating eggs or meat on their own.
- **Exception: milk** already contains carb, protein and fat in balance (about 8 g of each per cup of whole milk).
- Example pairings:
  - eggs + orange juice
  - fruit + cheese
  - milk + fruit
  - potatoes + butter + fish
  - coffee + cream + sugar

### 3.3 Calories
- The book's approach is "eat more, work out less." It is "not a diet."
- **Increase food slowly.** Jumping calories quickly causes weight gain.
- Author example (120 lb woman):
  - **1,750–1,900 kcal** on rest days
  - **2,000–2,200 kcal** on training days
  - more when training for an event
- Case example: a client went from 1,200–1,500 kcal to about 2,500 kcal over 3 months. She gained some weight while healing, then settled at 1,800–2,000 kcal and was leaner by 6 months.
- Front-load calories. In a case example, the extra 500 kcal/day was **all added before dinner**: bigger breakfast and lunch, smaller dinner. "Breakfast and lunch set the tone" for the day's metabolic rate.

### 3.4 Expected course
1. The body **warms up** and symptoms improve first: energy, sleep, digestion, mood.
2. **Weight loss usually comes last.** Some people gain weight at first, especially coming off low-carb, low-calorie or over-exercise.
3. The timeline runs from **3 months to several years**. It is an ongoing practice, not a destination.

## 4. Blood-sugar balancing protocol

### 4.1 Basics
- The normal range is about **65–105 mg/dL**.
- **Too low** comes from long gaps between meals, stress, over-exercise, dieting, caffeine or alcohol without food. The body responds with adrenaline, cortisol and glucagon, which break down tissue.
- **Too high** comes from too much food, the wrong macros or eating too fast. That causes an insulin spike: glycogen fills first, then the excess goes to fat.

### 4.2 Rules
1. **Size meals right.**
   - Most meals are **300–700 kcal**; snacks are **100–300 kcal**.
   - A meal should hold hunger off for **3–4 hours**.
   - Afterward the person should feel alert, happy and warm, not stuffed, sleepy or wired.
2. **Set meal frequency by liver glycogen capacity.**
   - **Most people: at least 6 eating occasions** (3 meals + 3 snacks).
   - **Significant metabolic issues** (hypoglycemia, diabetes, sugar sensitivity): **8–10 small meals**.
   - Space meals further apart as the liver heals.
3. **Eat when demand is high** (working, training, thinking). **Eat less when it is low** (lounging, late evening).
4. **Eat slowly and mindfully.**
   - Take **20–30 minutes per meal** and chew each bite **10–15 times**.
   - No eating while driving or in front of a screen.
5. **Remove** processed food and recreational drugs while healing.
6. **Alcohol**
   - Alcohol on its own drops blood sugar. Avoid it while healing.
   - If drinking, do it with food and/or with **fruit juice**. Per the book, fructose clears alcohol from the liver about 80% faster.
7. **Coffee and tea**
   - Never drink them black on an empty stomach. Add milk or cream plus sugar.
   - Jitters, nausea or anxiety after coffee mean the drink needs more sugar and fat.
8. **No "nothing after 7 pm" rule.** Fasting 3–4 hours before bed depletes liver glycogen and causes night waking. Use a bedtime snack (§9.2).

### 4.3 Sugar-sensitive person (SSP) protocol
- Cutting out all sugar is a short-term fix that worsens dysfunction long term. The body will make glucose from its own tissue anyway.
- Add the right carbs back **slowly**, always paired with fat and protein.
- Start with the easiest sugars: pulp-free OJ, honey, cooked fruit, milk.

### 4.4 Headaches
- These often come from unbalanced blood sugar or too little good sugar and food.
- Fix: many small, balanced meals.

## 5. Carbohydrates

### 5.1 Position
- Carbohydrates are **the body's preferred and most efficient fuel**.
- **Simple sugars are best**: fruit, pulp-free juice, honey, milk, and even white sugar. Starches and grains are second-tier.
- The reasons the book gives:
  - **Brain:** glucose is its main fuel; it can use up to half of available glucose.
  - **Red blood cells and retina:** these use glucose only.
  - **Muscle:** glycogen fuels lifting and sprints. Without it, muscle is broken down.
  - **Thyroid:** the liver needs glucose to convert **T4 → active T3**. The book says this is why low-carb dieters run cold.
  - **Liver detox:** needs glucose and glycogen. Fasts and cleanses that cut fruit, juice and milk hinder the liver.

### 5.2 Glycemic index argument
- Starches are pure glucose chains and enter the blood faster than fruit or even table sugar.
- Fruit, honey and juice contain **fructose + glucose**. Fructose enters slowly, barely affects insulin, and slows the glucose.

| Food | GI (as cited) |
|---|---|
| Glucose | 100 |
| Wheat bread | 78 |
| Sucrose (table sugar) | 65 |
| Orange juice | 52 |
| Milk | ~35 |
| Fructose | ~11 |

- The author also claims fructose enters cells without insulin, improves insulin sensitivity, increases CO₂ and energy, and improves magnesium retention.
- **Potassium**, from OJ, fruit and potatoes, helps move sugar into cells and blunts swings.

### 5.3 Best carbohydrates (Appendix A — all best organic)

**Fruits**
- pulp-free orange juice
- watermelon, papaya, kiwi, pineapple
- cooked apples or applesauce, cooked pears, cooked peaches
- ripe, mushy or cooked bananas
- nectarines, oranges, cherries, apricots, plums, grapes
- prunes, dates, raisins
- lemons, limes
- coconut water
- lychees, sapote, pawpaw

**Vegetables**
- \*russet potatoes; \*light-colored (yellow) sweet potatoes
- raw carrots
- beets, cucumbers, squash, zucchini, pumpkin
- onion; red, green and yellow peppers
- bamboo shoots

\* **Always add a fat to starches** for digestibility. All other vegetables are for moderation, well cooked and eaten with fat.

**Dairy**
- cow's milk (1%, 2%, whole), goat's milk, raw milk
- organic pastured is best
- Milk counts as carb, protein and fat.

**Grains** (only in moderation, only with good gut health, always with a fat)
- organic white or jasmine rice
- slow-cooked oatmeal
- sourdough bread
- masa harina

**Other**
- raw honey
- white sugar

### 5.4 Carbs to avoid
- high-fructose corn syrup and **agave**
- soda, sports drinks and energy drinks
- cookies, cakes, candy, crackers and frozen desserts
- bread, pasta and most grain products
- artificial sweeteners (§7.4)

### 5.5 Juice rules
- Unless the person's metabolism is already high, **pair juice with fat and protein**: OJ + gelatin, OJ + milk, OJ + eggs.
- Use **pulp-free** juice. Fresh-squeezed and strained through cheesecloth is best, because pulp fiber feeds gut bacteria.
- If someone hasn't been eating sugar or juice, **add them slowly**. Too much too fast causes weight gain, hormone issues, a rise in cholesterol and blood-sugar problems.

### 5.6 Potatoes
- Healthy people may eat them freely.
- For **gut healing or fat loss**: no more than **once a day**, preferably **at breakfast or lunch**.
- Wash and drain the cut potatoes to remove surface starch, cook thoroughly, and eat with fat.
- Russet or yellow sweet potato is easiest. Orange sweet potato and yams are harder to digest because of their high carotene.

### 5.7 Gut-friendly sugars for a damaged gut
- pulp-free OJ
- honey
- **cooked, skinless** apples, pears, peaches and nectarines
- watermelon
- papaya and other tropical fruit

With gut problems, **eat less fiber, not more.**

## 6. Fats

### 6.1 Saturated fats: the preferred fats
- Saturated fats have single carbon bonds, so they are stable: **no rancidity or breakdown with heat or oxygen**. Cook with them.
- Fat should be **about 20–30% of calories**, mostly saturated. Eat them in moderation, not a stick of butter in a night.
- The book's evidence, in brief:
  - It critiques Ancel Keys's Seven Countries work as cherry-picked.
  - Framingham: 50% of heart-attack patients had low cholesterol.
  - A 2010 AJCN meta-analysis of about 350,000 people found no saturated fat–CVD link.
  - Traditional high-saturated-fat cultures (Tokelau, Maasai, Inuit) have low heart disease.

**Best fats (Appendix C), ranked**
1. **Coconut oil**: refined, organic, expeller-pressed. This is the author's #1 takeaway.
   - It is made of MCTs, which need no bile salts and go straight to the liver as quick energy.
   - Its lauric acid is described as antifungal, antibacterial and antiviral.
   - Refined is preferred because it is gentler on the gut.
2. **Grass-fed butter**: ingredients should be only cream and salt. Contains butyric acid and vitamins A, D, E and K2.
3. **Grass-fed ghee**: high smoke point, good for frying. Rich in vitamins A and K2.
4. **Raw cacao / dark chocolate**: magnesium, antioxidants and stearic acid. Chocolate ingredients should be **only cacao, sugar, vanilla and salt**, with no soy lecithin or gums.
5. **Beef lard**

**Moderate:** extra-virgin olive oil (monounsaturated). **Use it as a dressing only; don't cook with it.**

### 6.2 Coconut oil dosing protocol
1. Start at **1 tsp/day**.
2. Add **1 tsp each week**.
3. Work up to **1–2 Tbsp/day**.

- Ways to use it: cooking, soups, broth, smoothies, dressing, or a spoonful on its own.
- Watch the response: warmer? better digestion? fewer illnesses?
- **If stools get too loose, cut back** until adapted.
- Replacing PUFA oils with coconut oil and butter can also start at about ½ Tbsp, adding a little each week. In a case example, temperature rose within 2 weeks.

### 6.3 PUFAs: the book's "toxic, not essential" position
- Double bonds make PUFAs unstable. They oxidize, create free radicals, and set off chain reactions.
- The book's claimed harms:
  - suppress the thyroid and the tissue response to thyroid hormone
  - damage mitochondria
  - inhibit protein-digesting enzymes ("malnourished while eating well")
  - suppress immunity
  - promote weight gain, aging and disease
- **Hibernation argument:** bears and squirrels fatten on high-PUFA nuts and seeds to slow their metabolism. Animals fed coconut oil plus carbs reportedly could not hibernate.
- **Livestock argument:** in the 1940s farmers found corn and soy oil fattened animals cheaply, while coconut oil made them lean.
- **"Essential fatty acid" history:** the Burrs' 1929 "EFA deficiency" in rats was later attributed to **B6 deficiency**. In the book's account, PUFA only "prevented" it by slowing metabolism.
- **Mead acid (omega-9):** the body makes it from saturated fat and sugar when dietary PUFA is low. The book calls it anti-inflammatory.
- **Omega-3/6 and fish oil:** the book says they lower cholesterol and inflammation **by suppressing the immune system and the liver's cholesterol synthesis**, which treats symptoms, not causes.
  - 2012 study, >70,000 patients: no mortality or cardiac benefit.
  - 2014 study, >600,000 people: no cardiovascular benefit.
  - Sydney Diet Heart Study: replacing saturated fat with linoleic acid increased deaths.
- **FDA (2002):** combined added EPA + DHA should not exceed **3 g/day**.

**PUFA oils to avoid (Appendix D — % omega-6)**

| Oil | Omega-6 % |
|---|---|
| Grapeseed | 70.6 |
| Corn | 54.5 |
| Walnut | 53.9 |
| Cottonseed | 52.4 |
| Soybean | 51.4 |
| Sesame | 42.0 |
| Peanut | 33.5 |
| Margarine | 27.9 |
| Chicken fat | 19.5 |
| Almond | 19.1 |
| Canola | 19.0 |
| Flaxseed | 12.9 |

Also avoid: safflower, sunflower, evening primrose, borage, **fish oil, cod liver oil**, and omega-3/omega-6 supplements.

**Restaurants:** most cook in vegetable oil. Ask for **butter or olive oil**, and limit eating out to **1–2 times a week**.

## 7. Protein

### 7.1 Position
- Protein is essential for structure, enzymes, hormones, transport proteins, antibodies and fluid balance.
- It is **a poor daily energy source**. Burning it produces ammonia, urea and lactic acid.
- **Too much protein** slows metabolism, raises stress hormones, disrupts sleep and breaks down muscle. **Too little** is equally stressful.
- Transport proteins (albumin, SHBG, thyroid-binding proteins) fall with **fasting, starvation and stress**, so hormones are delivered less well.

### 7.2 How much
- RDA minimum: 0.8 g/kg. Active people may need 2–3 times that.
- **Ray Peat:** at least **80 g/day**; **130–150 g/day** for an active, high-metabolism person.
- **Author:** 100–120 g/day at 120 lb and active, about 25% of calories.
- **Hypometabolic clients** have low HCl and digest protein poorly. Prioritize **easy-to-digest** protein.
- **Plant-based eaters** may need more total protein, because anti-nutrients reduce digestibility.

**Protein guidelines for a healthy person (g/day)**

| Body weight | Sedentary | Active |
|---|---|---|
| 100–125 lb | 60–90 | 90–125 |
| 126–150 lb | 70–100 | 100–150 |
| 151–175 lb | 80–110 | 110–175 |
| 176–200 lb | 100–120 | 120–200 |
| >200 lb | 120+ | 180+ |

**Signs protein is about right:**
- warm body (~98.6 °F) and pulse 75–90
- good digestion and sleep
- good muscle tone, libido and mood
- blood pressure about 120/80
- healthy hair, skin, nails, bones and teeth

If these are off, check protein, but also check the carb and fat balance.

### 7.3 Inflammatory / "anti-metabolic" amino acids
These are high in muscle meat and egg whites and are released when muscle breaks down.

| Amino acid | Book's claim | High in |
|---|---|---|
| **Tryptophan** | Converts to serotonin under stress, which raises ACTH and cortisol and suppresses the thyroid | soy, egg whites, elk, seaweed, liver, whey |
| **Cysteine** | Inhibits thyroid peroxidase, which lowers T4 production | muscle meat |
| **Methionine** | Thyroid-suppressing; methionine-restricted rats lived 43% longer | egg whites, seeds, meat, grains |
| **Histidine** | Precursor to histamine; becomes histamine in aged, fermented or spoiled foods | wine, kefir, yogurt, sauerkraut, fermented fish, sausage, ground meat |

- **Histamine-intolerance signs:** chronic cough, sneezing, watery eyes, breathing issues. Reduce fermented and aged foods.
- **Gelatin and bone broth contain no tryptophan** and little cysteine, methionine or histidine. They balance meat and add glycine, proline and alanine.

### 7.4 Super Proteins (should make up the majority of protein)
**Appendix B frequencies are in bold.**

| Protein | How much / how often | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Milk and dairy** | **2–6 servings/day**. The author has about 4 servings plus other dairy, roughly ⅓ of her protein. | Whole milk has ~8 g protein per cup and ~33 g per quart. Additive-free cheese: Parmigiano-Reggiano, ricotta. See §8. |
| **Whole eggs** (pastured, organic, soy- and corn-free) | **Up to 2/day** | Eat the yolk. The protein split is 60% white, 40% yolk, but nearly all the nutrients are in the yolk. Pair with OJ or fruit, because eggs strongly stimulate insulin. Conventional eggs only: limit to **2–3 per week**. |
| **Grass-fed beef liver** | **3–6 oz once every 7–10 days** | 25 g protein per 4 oz; very high in vitamins A and B12, plus copper, zinc and selenium. "4 oz liver ≈ 5 lb fruit" in nutrients. More is not better, because of its tryptophan. Cook in coconut oil or ghee and eat with cooked fruit or vegetables. |
| **Shellfish**: oysters, shrimp, scallops, mussels, clams, crab, lobster | **1–3×/week** (1–2× in Ch. 8) | Zinc, copper, selenium, B12. **Always cook** (the main foodborne-illness risk is raw shellfish). The FDA allows up to 12 oz/week. Cook in butter and serve with cooked vegetables or fruit. |
| **Warm-water / low-fat white fish**: sole, cod, whitefish, tilapia, flounder, catfish | **2–4×/week** | Low in PUFA. **Fatty fish** (salmon, mackerel, herring, albacore) only **1–2×/month**, fried in butter or coconut oil. |
| **Potato protein** | — | 2 lb well-cooked potato ≈ the protein of 1 L milk (~33 g). Juiced potato protein suits a very compromised metabolism or vegetarians. Recipe in §15. |
| **Bone broth** | **1–3 cups/day** (healthy 1–2; hypometabolic 3–4+) | ~8 g protein per cup. **Always 1 cup with any muscle-meat meal.** |
| **Powdered gelatin** (Great Lakes) | **1–6 Tbsp/day** | Add to OJ, coffee, milk, smoothies, soups and sauces. |

**Share of protein from broth + gelatin:** healthy **25–35%**; hypometabolic or poor digestion **up to 50%**.

**Gelatin types**
- **Bovine or porcine (regular):** thickens. Dissolve it in warm liquid first. Use for gummies, custards, soups and sauces.
- **Hydrolyzed (collagen hydrolysate):** dissolves in cold liquid and **does not thicken**. Best for OJ, smoothies, coffee and workout drinks.
- Gelatin is **not a complete protein** and not ideal for muscle building. For muscle gain, use dairy, eggs, fish, shellfish and meat.

**Glycine** (abundant in gelatin) is used for:
- glutathione, creatine and bile salts
- liver recovery
- gastric acid secretion
- sleep, memory and calm (it is an inhibitory neurotransmitter)

Get it from food, not isolated glycine supplements.

### 7.5 Muscle meats (beef, bison, lamb, chicken, turkey, pork, venison)
- The book's concerns:
  - high in inflammatory amino acids
  - high phosphorus-to-calcium ratio, which raises PTH and pulls calcium from bone
  - conventional meat is grain- and soy-fed, with antibiotics and hormones
- **Poultry and pork** are non-ruminants, so their fat carries more PUFA. Pasture-raised is better than organic, because organic birds still eat corn and soy. The author eats chicken about once a month.
- **Grass-fed ruminants** (beef, bison, lamb) are preferred, but still in moderation.
- **Frequency:**
  - Healthy: **2–3×/week** (Ch. 10). Appendix B says **1×/week or less** if inflamed.
  - **Hypometabolic, diabetes, arthritis/autoimmune, gout, cancer or other inflammatory disease:** eliminate muscle meat or save it for special occasions until health improves.
- **Rule:** always eat muscle meat with **1 cup of bone broth or 1 Tbsp of gelatin**.
- **Step-down protocol** (case example): go from meat 3×/day to 2×/day, replacing each removed meat meal with broth + coconut oil + Parmesan + fruit. Over about 4 months, reach **3×/week**. Gut symptoms improve first, then joints, allergies, sleep and energy.
- Sources: U.S. Wellness Meats (GrasslandBeef.com), Tropical Traditions.

### 7.6 Protein powders
- These are processed. Heat, solvents and alkalis damage the amino acids. Powders often contain:
  - gums and carrageenan
  - hexane-extracted soy lecithin
  - synthetic vitamins
  - artificial sweeteners
  - heavy metals (Consumer Reports, 2010: all 15 products tested had at least one)
  - unlabeled MSG
- **Whey:** a cheese-making by-product. It lacks milk's protective factors, is high in tryptophan, and its cholesterol oxidizes when heated.
- **Rice, hemp, soy and pea protein:** not recommended. You'd need about 2× the serving.
- **Only recommended powder:** hydrolyzed collagen or gelatin from **Great Lakes Gelatin**.
- **Artificial sweeteners** (aspartame, sucralose, saccharin) are to be avoided. **Stevia** is the least bad, but protein + stevia without sugar still triggers a stress response. **Add real sugar to shakes.**
- Use powders sparingly. "Real food is best — always."

### 7.7 Nuts and seeds
- The book calls them anti-metabolic. Its reasons:
  - high PUFA (walnuts worst)
  - high oxalates: **42–469 mg/100 g**. Roasted almonds are highest; raw macadamia lowest.
  - phytates, which also block minerals from the *other* foods eaten at the same time
  - lectins and trypsin inhibitors
  - 25 g of protein from nuts costs about 600 kcal
- Raw nuts are hard to digest. Oil-roasted nuts add PUFA. Dry-roasted is a compromise.
- **Frequency:** **1–2×/month** if desired, more only with good gut health. Lightly roasted, soaked or sprouted macadamia, hazelnut or cashew are best.
- **Snack swap:** Parmesan or ricotta + fruit instead of nuts.
- Nuts are not a protein source.

### 7.8 Legumes and soy
**Legumes**
- They are 10–25% protein but contain lectins, phytates, trypsin inhibitors, oxalates and PUFA.
- Least problematic: **cooked fresh green beans and peas**. Hardest: kidney, lima and black beans.
- Canned beans, even organic, are not soaked.
- **Preparation:**
  1. Soak **1 cup of beans in 5 cups of warm water overnight**.
  2. Discard the soaking water.
  3. Cook thoroughly.
  4. Eat with butter, ghee or coconut oil.
- Soaking or sprouting cuts phytates 40–70%. Cooking alone leaves about 92%.
- **Frequency while healing: 1–2×/month at most**, or avoid.

**Soy** is the book's "most degraded legume."
- **Goitrogens** block iodine uptake.
- **Isoflavones** are phytoestrogens that inhibit thyroid peroxidase.
- It is very high in phytates and contains trypsin inhibitors and hemagglutinin.
- About 95% of US soy is genetically modified.
- The FDA suggests 25 g/day of soy. By comparison, Asians eat about 3–8 g/day of fermented soy.
- **Avoid:**
  - soy milk, soy cheese, soy burgers and soy sausage
  - tofu and edamame
  - soy protein, soy lecithin, soy oil and soy flour
  - soy bars, soy snacks and soy creamer
- **Allowed sparingly:** non-GMO fermented soy (tempeh, miso, natto, gluten-free soy sauce), e.g. tempeh 1–2×/month.

**Swap protocol**

| Swap out | Swap in |
|---|---|
| Soy milk | Organic milk |
| Soy protein | Gelatin |
| Tofu and soy burgers | Fish, eggs, occasional tempeh |
| Soy snacks | Fruit + cheese |

Plus **2 raw carrots a day** (§11.2).

## 8. Dairy (the book's "perfect food")

### 8.1 Why milk
- Whole milk is about 87% water, 4.6% lactose, 3.4% protein and 4.2% fat.
- Its fat is about 70% saturated and **<2.5% PUFA**.
- **About 8 g each of carb, protein and fat per cup**, which makes it a natural blood-sugar regulator.
- Rich in calcium, magnesium, potassium, selenium, and vitamins A, B2, B12, D and K.
- **Calcium-to-phosphorus ratio:** dairy is high in calcium.
  - A diet near **2:1 Ca:P** improves calcium absorption and **lowers parathyroid hormone (PTH)**.
  - Chronic high PTH breaks down bone, and per Peat it lowers metabolic rate by reducing mitochondrial uncoupling.
  - High-phosphorus, low-calcium foods to limit: muscle meat, grains, soy, seeds, Brazil nuts, colas.
- **Associations cited:** better bone density, lower body fat and better body composition, lower blood pressure, less metabolic syndrome, and an inverse link with colon and breast cancer.

### 8.2 Choosing milk (best → acceptable)
1. **Raw organic pastured milk**, where legal and tolerated
2. **Organic pastured, non-homogenized, additive-free whole milk**
3. Organic pastured pasteurized milk. Ultra-pasteurized is fine for guts that don't tolerate raw.

- Avoid:
  - **carrageenan** (in many low-fat, chocolate and plant milks)
  - **dried milk powder** added to skim and low-fat milk (it creates oxysterols)
  - **synthetic vitamin A and D fortification**
  - **rBGH**
- Unfortified options: raw milk and some pasteurized whole milks. **Ask the producer.**
- Find sources at **RealMilk.com**.
- **Plant "milks"** (almond, rice, soy) are not milk: they are high in PUFA, fortified, and contain gums. Avoid them.
- **Whole milk is usually better tolerated** than reduced-fat. Some "milk allergies" are really reactions to additives.

### 8.3 Milk by goal

| Goal | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| General health | Least processed, whole, organic pastured milk |
| Post-workout | **Lower-fat milk + sugar, honey or cacao.** Fat slows glycogen refill; chocolate milk is well supported for recovery. |
| Fat loss | **3–4 cups/day of full-fat milk can stall fat loss.** Use additive-free 1–2% milk. |

### 8.4 Lactose-intolerance reintroduction protocol
The book attributes much intolerance to small-intestine damage from grains, PUFA, alcohol, drugs and additives, which lowers lactase.

1. **Heal the gut first** (Ray Peat says this can take as little as about 2 weeks).
   - Remove grains, nuts, raw vegetables and legumes.
   - Add bone broth, coconut oil, gelatin and a daily raw carrot.
2. Test **additive-free cheese** first (aged cheese has little lactose): Parmigiano-Reggiano, ricotta.
3. Start milk at **1–2 oz once a day, every day**.
4. **Add about 1 oz/day each week** until reaching the goal. In the case example, the client went up 2 oz/week to 3–4 cups/day.

A survey cited in the book: 81% of self-reported lactose-intolerant people had no symptoms on raw milk.

### 8.5 Cheese and yogurt
- **Cheese:** choose the fewest ingredients. Avoid GMO cultures and rennet, cellulose, coloring, "natural flavors," gums and added whey. Favored: **Parmigiano-Reggiano and ricotta**. *[The book's "top four cheeses" list is not in the source.]*
- **Yogurt:** fine in small amounts but not a main protein or calcium source.
  - Per Peat, its lactic acid burdens the liver.
  - Not for people who are hypoglycemic, hypometabolic or have an overloaded liver.
  - If eaten, choose **plain 2% Greek yogurt** (e.g. Fage).

## 9. Grains and gluten

### 9.1 Position
- **Avoid all grains while healing a damaged metabolism.** The book's reasons:
  - Starch is pure glucose. Wheat bread raises blood sugar faster than sugar.
  - Grains have a high phosphorus-to-calcium ratio.
  - They contain oxalates (buckwheat and whole wheat worst; white rice lowest), phytates, trypsin inhibitors and lectins.
  - They contain gluten and gluten-like prolamines. The book counts corn, rice and quinoa proteins as capable of similar reactions.
- **Celiac/gluten-sensitivity symptoms:**
  - fatigue, brain fog, depression
  - diarrhea or constipation, bloating, gas
  - weight loss or gain
  - skin issues
  - joint or back pain
  - thin bones
- **Hidden gluten:**
  - beer, soy sauce, ketchup, sauces, dressings
  - ice cream, some candy
  - lunch meat, bouillon cubes, seitan
  - seasoned rice and chips, fried foods
  - cosmetics
- **Processed "gluten-free" products are not health foods.** They are full of gums, soy and synthetic vitamins, and about 30% exceed gluten limits anyway.

### 9.2 30-day grain elimination test
1. Remove **all** grains, grain products, gluten-free products and gluten-containing additives for **at least 30 days**. Reducing isn't enough.
2. Reintroduce and observe.
3. No change means the person likely tolerates grains. Even then, never 6–9 servings/day.

**Grains allowed once healthy (moderation, always with fat):** white or jasmine rice, slow-cooked oatmeal, sourdough, masa harina.

## 10. Vegetables

### 10.1 Position
- **Above-ground and leafy vegetables carry plant defenses:**
  - oxalates, which block calcium (spinach and kale are poor calcium sources)
  - lectins
  - lignans (phytoestrogens; worst in flax, sesame and cruciferous vegetables)
  - **goitrogens** (cruciferous vegetables, soy)
  - pyridoxine glucoside, which cuts B6 availability 75–85%
  - pectin and cellulose, which feed bacteria and raise endotoxin and serotonin in a damaged gut
- **Root vegetables** grow below ground and carry fewer defenses.

### 10.2 Rules
- **Cook vegetables (and fruit) well.**
  - Cooking breaks down pectin, lignin, lectins and goitrogens.
  - **Cook cruciferous vegetables about 30 minutes** to reduce the goitrogenic effect.
  - Raw carrot is the exception (§11.2).
- **Always eat with fat and protein**, preferably butter or coconut oil.
- **Best vegetables:**
  - roots: potato, light sweet potato, beets, turnips, carrots
  - "fruit-vegetables": squash, zucchini, pumpkin, tomato, cucumber, peppers
- **Salads:** the author went from salads 2–3×/day to **2–3×/month**. A gut-friendly salad is **raw carrot, tomato, cucumber, onion and peppers** with olive oil and vinegar.
- **Who should consider cutting leafy and cruciferous greens:** people with digestive issues, gas or bloating, sugar or salt cravings, or allergies.

### 10.3 The fiber argument (Monastyrsky, *Fiber Menace*)
- Gut damage kills beneficial bacteria, so stools harden. Adding fiber bulks the stool but doesn't heal the gut, and may irritate an inflamed lining.
- **Gut-healing swap:**
  - fewer raw salads and grains
  - more bone broth and gelatin
  - cook all fruit and vegetables in butter or coconut oil
  - add eggs and dairy
- In a case example, bloating resolved in about 2 weeks and stools normalized by about 6 months.

## 11. "Real food supplements" and nutrient sources

### 11.1 Stance
- The book is against most pills.
- High-dose multivitamins showed no mortality, CVD or cancer benefit in a study of more than 400,000 people.
- Supplements don't heal and can mask problems. **Use food as the supplement.**
- Exception mentioned: liquid vitamin D3 (Carlson's) if sun and dairy are inadequate.

### 11.2 The raw carrot
- Eat **1–2 raw carrots a day**, grated as a carrot salad (recipe §15) or whole.
- **Purpose, per the book:**
  - Its indigestible fiber binds and clears endotoxin and excess estrogen from the gut.
  - It supports gut healing.
  - It replaces a daily green salad.
- Used in cases of estrogen dominance, soy history and gut issues.

### 11.3 Salt
- **Claimed benefits:** lowers stress hormones and inflammation, raises metabolic rate and heat, helps sleep, improves muscle function, and helps wound healing.
- **The book's argument against restriction:**
  - Low salt raises noradrenaline and activates the RAAS.
  - Studies cited: Alderman (1995 and 1998), a Belgian study (2011), and NEJM 2014 with more than 100,000 people.
  - In the NEJM study, **3,000–6,000 mg sodium/day** had the lowest death and CVD rates. **Under 3,000 mg** had about 27% higher risk.
- **How much:** salt food **to taste**; it shouldn't taste overly salty.
  - Add a pinch to OJ, milk, gelatin drinks and workout drinks.
  - Increase **slowly**. Too fast causes water retention and bloating.
- **Who:**
  - People already eating whole food, with good habits, benefit.
  - People eating mostly processed food (already high in salt) should clean up the diet first.
  - **Refer clients with hypertension or kidney disease to their provider.**
- **Type:** clean **white** salt, not pink or gray. The book recommends **Morton's Canning & Pickling Salt** (non-iodized).

### 11.4 Coffee
- **Benefits claimed:**
  - liver protection; 40–50% lower risk of liver cancer at 3+ cups/day
  - lower liver enzymes
  - thyroid support
  - relief of constipation
  - links to lower diabetes and Parkinson's risk
- Coffee contains magnesium, potassium, B1 and B3. The research benefits are at **3–5 cups/day**.
- **Must come with calories.** Caffeine raises glucose use, so black coffee on an empty stomach drops blood sugar and causes jitters or anxiety.
- **Eat before and after coffee, and sip slowly.**
- **Iron:** coffee blocks non-heme iron absorption.
  - Iron-deficient clients should drink coffee **at least 1 hour away from iron-rich meals**.
  - It can help with iron overload.
- **Author's coffee recipe:**
  - 10 oz organic dark roast
  - 2–3 Tbsp cream
  - 2 Tbsp white sugar
  - 1 Tbsp hydrolyzed gelatin
  - optional pinch of salt
  - Sip through the morning.

### 11.5 Eggshell calcium
- **For whom:** people who don't eat enough dairy. Dairy is the preferred calcium source.
- An eggshell is about 90% calcium carbonate, plus trace minerals. One shell (~6 g) holds about 2,400 mg calcium.
- **Doses:**
  - ⅛ tsp ≈ 1 g calcium carbonate ≈ 400 mg calcium
  - ¼ tsp ≈ 600 mg calcium carbonate, as listed in the recipe
- RDA is about 1,300 mg/day; Ray Peat suggests about 2,400 mg.
- **On a low-dairy diet: ¼ tsp, 1–3×/day, with food or OJ.** Calcium alone can irritate an empty stomach.
- Absorption equals calcium citrate and is not dependent on stomach acid (study cited). It has lower heavy metals than oyster-shell calcium.
- Recipe in §15.

### 11.6 Mineral broth
- **1–2 shots daily**, for magnesium, vitamin K and B vitamins. Recipe in §15.

## 12. Hydration

- **Drink to thirst.** The book rejects "8 glasses," "1 gallon" or "half your body weight."
- **Overhydration** without electrolytes dilutes sodium. Effects:
  - frequent clear urination (the author goes 5–6×/day; an overhydrated friend went about 20×)
  - bloating, night urination, headaches, cramps, feeling cold
  - stress on the thyroid
- **Fix:** shift fluid toward **mineral-rich drinks** (milk, OJ, bone broth, coconut water) and reduce plain water. Thirst and bathroom trips usually drop.
- **Heat, humidity and exercise:**
  - Drink more, but add **salt and sugar** (and magnesium, calcium and potassium).
  - Replacing sweat with plain water risks **hyponatremia**. In a Boston Marathon study, 13% of finishers were hyponatremic; risk factors were >3 L of fluid and a ~4-hour finish.

## 13. Lifestyle: stress, sleep, exercise, happiness

### 13.1 Stress (Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome)
1. **Alarm:** freeze, or fight-or-flight.
2. **Resistance:** fat and muscle breakdown and glucose mobilization. The body recovers if the stress ends.
3. **Exhaustion:** fatigue, depression, weight change, immune and digestive problems, disease.
   - What's called "adrenal fatigue" is, per the author, really **suppressed thyroid**.

The body adds all stressors together: diet, training, work, relationships. "A good diet will only go so far" with 80-hour weeks or an unhappy life.

### 13.2 Sleep
- **Target: 7–9 hours of deep sleep.** Average US sleep has fallen from >8 hours (1940s) to <7 hours.
- **Darkness is stressful and catabolic.**
  - Ignoring tiredness and getting a "second wind" (tired at 9, wired at 10:30) is stress hormones kicking in.
  - **Go to bed when tired.** "Going to bed earlier may be all the medicine you need."
- **Deep sleep** builds mitochondria and stem cells (Peat), and muscles burn mostly fat during sleep.
- **Night-waking mechanism:**
  1. Low thyroid leaves the liver short of glycogen.
  2. Blood sugar falls overnight.
  3. Adrenaline and cortisol rise.
  4. The person wakes, often around 2–4 am, or can't fall asleep.
- **Bedtime snack protocol** (eat before bed; also usable on night waking):
  - 8 oz milk + 1 Tbsp honey + pinch of salt, **or**
  - 6 oz OJ + pinch of salt + 1 Tbsp hydrolyzed gelatin, **or**
  - 8 oz warm bone broth + salt + ½ cup fruit, **or**
  - 4 oz milk + 4 oz OJ + ½ Tbsp gelatin + salt
- A steady flow of sugar, protein, fat and salt through the day lowers evening stress hormones.
- *[The book's "10 simple things you can do now" for sleep are not in the source.]*

### 13.3 Exercise
**Core claim:** exercise is a stressor. Strenuous cardio *and* heavy or high-intensity training are associated with lower thyroid function.
- HIIT, CrossFit and similar training don't raise resting metabolic rate. The extra burn comes from work plus adrenaline and cortisol.
- A healthy body recovers fast, especially with sugar and minerals afterward. A damaged one stays suppressed.

**While healing**
- If temperature and pulse are very low and there are multiple symptoms: **stop or sharply cut exercise** until diet, rest and lower stress improve things.
- Otherwise, **light to moderate only:**
  - walking, gentle (not hot) yoga, easy swimming, tai chi, light weights
  - **30–45 minutes**
  - **heart rate under 100 bpm**
  - in a **cool, naturally lit room** (heat and darkness are stressors)
- **Avoid:** running, intense hiking, HIIT, heavy lifting.
- **Self-check:**
  - A good session leaves you feeling better all day.
  - Feeling drained 1–2 hours later means too much.
  - A temperature or pulse drop afterward means too much.
- Focus on energy, sleep, digestion, mood and hormones, **not weight loss**.
- The author's own healing phase: 2-hour workouts became 2-hour naps. She slept more, ate more and moved less.

**When healthy**
- **No more than 2–3 intense sessions/week**, with full recovery.
- Training intensely 5–7 days/week leads to over-training and a depressed thyroid.
- **Moderate weight training** builds muscle, which means more fat burned at rest. The author's return: **≤45 minutes, 4–5×/week**.
- *[The book's "quick guidelines" for weight training are not in the source.]*

**Workout fueling**
- **Before** a 30–60 minute session: **200–400 kcal**, 30–60 minutes ahead. Never train fasted.
  - Examples: eggs + OJ, fruit + cheese, a milk smoothie.
- **During:** optional if fed beforehand. Sip **Kate's Miracle Drink** (§15): sugar, gelatin, salt and minerals.
  - In winter, warm broth works as a pre-, during- or post-workout drink.
- **After:** muscles are insulin-sensitive for **up to 2 hours**. Have carbs + protein.
  - If no meal is coming, use a milk drink: homemade chocolate milk, milk + honey + salt, or an orange creamsicle.
  - Or: broth + pinch of salt + coconut oil + 1 cup OJ.

### 13.4 Happiness
- Unhappiness is a major chronic stressor.
- Choices count: food, exercise, people, job and workload, sleep, free time, how you react.
- *Blue Zones* (Buettner): the longest-lived communities share **low stress and strong community**.
- Jeanne Calment (122 years) credited laughter and not stressing.
- Coaching prompt: "Do you truly want to change?" If a job, relationship or habit causes more stress than joy, something may need to change.
- *[The book's "Top 10 tips to find happiness" are not in the source.]*

### Foundations

**Beef Bone Broth** — per cup ~8 g protein, 36 kcal
- **Ingredients:**
  - 4 lb raw grass-fed beef bones (knuckle, oxtail, neck, shank)
  - 1–2 chicken feet (for more gel)
  - 2 Tbsp vinegar or lemon juice
  - 2 Tbsp OJ
  - 1 Tbsp salt
  - water to cover
  - chopped vegetables
- **Method** (from the book's text):
  1. Cover the bones with water, acid and salt.
  2. Simmer **12–72 hours**. Chicken bones need only about 12 hours.
  3. Add the vegetables in the **last 3–4 hours**.
  4. Strain, refrigerate, and **skim off the fat**.
- Gelling can take 48–72 hours of simmering.
- **Storage:** 5 days refrigerated, 2–3 months frozen, in glass or BPA-free containers.
- **Avoid store-bought broth.** It contains MSG and additives, is made from skins, and lacks minerals.

**Mineral Broth** — essentially 0 kcal
- **Ingredients:**
  - ½ lb kale
  - ½ lb spinach
  - ½ lb chard
  - 2 cloves garlic
  - 1 Tbsp salt
  - 1 gallon filtered water
- *Method (reconstructed):* simmer for several hours, strain, and discard the greens. Drink only the liquid.
- Store in glass up to 2 weeks.
- **Take 1–2 shots daily** for magnesium, vitamin K and B vitamins.

**Eggshell Calcium**
- **Ingredients:** 12–24 eggshells from pastured, organic hens.
- **Method** (from the book's text): **boil, then bake** until dry, then **grind to a fine powder**. Never use raw shells.
- Store sealed in glass.
- **Dose:** ¼ tsp ≈ 600 mg calcium carbonate. Take with food or OJ.

**Juiced Potato Protein** (very compromised digestion; vegetarians) — ~33 g protein
1. Juice **2 lb white (russet) potatoes**.
2. Let it stand until the starch settles, then pour off the juice.
3. Cook the juice in a pan with a little butter, scrambling until it sets.
4. Season with salt and pepper. It tastes like mashed potato.

**Broth meal** (gut-healing or low-appetite meal): bone broth + coconut oil + Parmesan + mashed potato.

## 17. Common client questions (from the book's FAQ)

- **"Will I gain weight?"**
  - Possibly at first, especially if coming from low-carb, low-calorie or over-exercise.
  - Slow changes minimize the gain.
  - Warmth, sleep and energy improve before weight drops.
- **"How long does it take?"** Three months to several years. It is ongoing, because new stressors keep appearing.
- **"How many changes at once?"** One or two. Build the habit, then add the next. Too many at once leads to overwhelm and quitting.
- **"I'm always thirsty and peeing."** Probably overhydrated and under-mineralized. Shift fluids to milk, OJ and broth, add salt, and reduce plain water.
- **"I feel hot when I eat more — is that normal?"** Yes. Food used as energy makes heat, and so does salt. It's a sign the metabolism is rising.
- **"How much bone broth?"** 1–2 cups/day, and 1 cup with any muscle meat. With very poor digestion, up to 50% of protein can come from broth and gelatin.
- **"My temperature and pulse dropped after my workout."** The session was too long, too hard or too intense. Cut duration or intensity, or add rest.
- **"Should I become a fat burner?"** No. Use glucose for activity. Fat is burned at rest and during sleep, and more muscle helps.
- **"Can I eat out?"** Yes, 1–2×/week. Ask for butter or olive oil, grass-fed meat and organic produce.
- **"Is juice too much sugar?"** Pair it with protein and fat unless the metabolism is already high. Add it slowly. Use pulp-free.
- **"Headaches?"** Usually blood sugar. Eat small, balanced meals more often and include enough good sugar.
- **"Potatoes every meal?"** Fine if healthy. For gut healing or fat loss, once a day at breakfast or lunch, rinsed, well cooked and with fat.
- **"Alcohol?"** Avoid while healing. Otherwise drink with food and/or juice.
- **"Where do I start?"** Slow, constant changes. Log daily. Work with a practitioner and ask your doctor questions.
